Why I am building this

For the past year I have been thinking about the steps a person in Ghana takes to get medical attention: finding a qualified doctor, arranging a consultation, getting a valid prescription, locating a pharmacy that has the medicine, arranging delivery, and knowing where to turn when urgent transport is needed. Today these steps are handled by separate people and services, and the patient is left to coordinate all of it.

I want to connect those steps in one organised journey, and give doctors, pharmacists and service partners mobile tools to deliver their part. I am not proposing to replace doctors, pharmacists, hospitals or emergency services. My aim is to make it easier for people to reach qualified providers, and easier for those providers to coordinate care.

I have used the year to challenge the idea, work through the practical problems and plan a version that can be built in phases and tested in the real world. The prototype below is the first visible result.

Five connected problems

  • AccessPatients may struggle to find an available, suitable professional and book an appointment.
  • FragmentationConsultation, prescription fulfilment, delivery and follow-up run as separate processes.
  • ConveniencePeople travel or make repeated calls just to learn availability, price or next steps.
  • Trust and accountabilityPatients and providers need clear records, transparent payments and a way to resolve order problems.
  • Urgent transportWhen medical transport is needed, patients need a verified provider and a clear escalation process.

How the platform works

One connected platform with role-based mobile experiences. Each step below is something you can try in the demo above.

  1. 1

    Connect

    A patient registers, searches for an available verified doctor and books a consultation. Unverified doctors cannot be booked.

  2. 2

    Consult

    The doctor assesses the patient remotely when clinically appropriate, records the encounter, and gives advice, a referral or a prescription when justified.

  3. 3

    Fulfil

    An authorised pharmacy verifies the prescription, confirms stock and price, and prepares the order.

  4. 4

    Deliver

    A suitable courier collects the order, gives status updates and records proof of delivery with an order-specific PIN.

  5. 5

    Protect

    The patient confirms receipt or raises a dispute. Payment settlement follows the agreed, provider-supported rules.

  6. 6

    Coordinate urgent transport

    The platform helps connect the patient or care team with a verified ambulance partner. It never shows a request as confirmed until a provider accepts it.

For medicine delivery I will work with courier partners rather than buy a fleet. For ambulance services I will partner with authorised providers and keep human dispatch oversight. Emergency calling must stay available even if the app or its integrations are down, which is why the emergency button sits at the top of the patient home screen.

A trust-centred payment protection model

One feature I consider essential is payment protection for pharmacy orders. The pharmacy's eligible payout stays pending until the agreed delivery and dispute conditions are met, to the extent supported by a payment provider's product and legal arrangement. Patients see the order total, delivery status, dispute window and refund status clearly. If a patient reports a missing, incorrect or damaged order, the relevant amount is held where possible while the case is reviewed, and the platform records the evidence and the resolution.

I will not assume that a normal payment gateway provides escrow, or that the company can legally hold customer funds itself. Both points must be confirmed with a suitably authorised provider and Ghanaian legal counsel.

What the prototype shows, and what it does not

What it demonstrates

  • One order moving across five roles, with each role seeing only its own tasks.
  • Unverified doctors and unapproved pharmacies blocked from taking part.
  • A doctor cannot close a consultation until the patient has joined and notes are written.
  • The payment states, held payouts and refunds, shown as the platform would record them.
  • Proof of delivery by PIN, failed delivery, and the inspection window.
  • Ambulance requests that stay unconfirmed until a provider accepts, with escalation to a human dispatcher.
  • A running audit trail of every step.

What it is not

  • Not a working medical service. No real doctors, prescriptions, payments, couriers or ambulances are involved.
  • Not proof of regulatory approval. Medical and Dental Council, Pharmacy Council and NePP, Data Protection Commission, Bank of Ghana and HeFRA requirements still have to be confirmed.
  • Not final pricing. Fees, delivery charges and the two-hour window are hypotheses to test.
  • Not the production build. The real app will be built in Flutter with a NestJS and PostgreSQL backend, as set out in the business plan.

How the business can earn revenue

I intend to build a sustainable business with transparent fees and written agreements. Potential revenue streams are:

  • A platform service fee, or agreed share, on consultations.
  • Permitted pharmacy transaction or technology fees.
  • Disclosed delivery coordination fees.
  • Future service agreements with employers, NGOs and institutions.

Ambulance-related fees would only be considered where lawful and contractually appropriate. I will not assume that publicly funded emergency services can be commercialised through commissions. The business will not sell patient information or influence clinical decisions to increase medicine sales.

Illustration, not a forecast: on a GHS 100 consultation with a 20% platform share, the platform earns GHS 20 and the doctor receives GHS 80. At 1,000 consultations a month that is GHS 20,000 in gross platform revenue, before payment fees, taxes, refunds, support, staffing and hosting. The demo's ledger uses the same 20% split.

Initial market and rollout

The first target markets are Accra and Kumasi. I will recruit a limited network of verified doctors, participating pharmacies and delivery partners, then test the service in defined coverage areas. The platform will not promise availability where a verified provider or delivery partner is not actually available.

The pilot aims to recruit 10–15 doctors, 5–10 pharmacies and 100–200 invited patients, and to reach at least 85% consultation completion, at least 90% fulfilment of accepted orders, full payment reconciliation and zero serious unresolved payment discrepancies at sign-off. The six-month schedule is a planning estimate, not a commitment.

My initial development budget is GHS 60,000–150,000. For planning I use GHS 120,000 as a working scenario, subject to quotations and regulatory discovery. Responsible implementation

Healthcare needs more than good software. Before public launch I will work with qualified Ghanaian advisers and the relevant authorities to confirm the requirements that apply to the platform's actual operating model.

  • Professional verification: verify clinicians and responsible pharmacists through the relevant professional and regulatory processes.
  • Electronic pharmacy compliance: confirm the Pharmacy Council and NePP onboarding, authorisation and integration pathway before enabling pharmacy transactions.
  • Data protection: confirm registration and compliance obligations, protect health information, apply least-privilege access and keep audit trails.
  • Payments: use a suitably authorised provider and confirm that delayed settlement, refunds and dispute holds are supported in the contract and applicable rules.
  • Ambulance safety: partner with appropriate providers, keep human oversight and keep direct emergency contact available. The plan proposes making 112 accessible, to be verified before launch.
  • Clinical safety: set up consent, documentation, prescribing, referral and escalation procedures with qualified professionals.
